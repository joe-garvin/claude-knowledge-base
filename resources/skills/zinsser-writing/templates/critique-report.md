# Critique report format

The structure for an itemized diagnosis in default mode. This is a format guide, not a rigid form to stamp onto every critique. Match the shape to the piece; a draft with one real problem doesn't need the full scaffolding.

## The shape

Open with one or two plain sentences locating the piece: what it is, what register, and the honest headline. Not a summary of the draft (the writer wrote it, they know what it says); the headline is the overall read. "This mostly works; two things are holding it back." Or: "The writing is clean line by line, but the lead is buried and the piece doesn't have a single center yet." Start on substance, no throat-clearing.

Then the itemized list, ordered by what matters most. Each item:

**[Principle].** Named plainly, a few words. The location quoted or pointed to exactly. The problem in a sentence or two: the specific failure here, not a lecture on the principle. The direction: where to take it, without writing the fix.

Keep each item tight enough to scan. The diagnosis shouldn't be longer than it needs to be.

Close by naming the rewrite option without assuming it: "Want me to take a pass, or revise from these?" Nothing more. No summary of the summary.

## Ordering

Lead with structure and clarity (buried lead, no unity, no single point, a sentence that can't be parsed). Then economy (clutter, qualifiers, passive constructions, dead adverbs and adjectives, concept nouns). Then sound and freshness (clichés, journalese, flat verbs, rhythm). Group related items so the writer isn't bouncing around the piece. Don't label tiers out loud or tag items "critical"; the order and the explanation carry the weight. Trust the writer as the reader.

## A filled shape, for reference

The example below is schematic; see `examples/worked-critique.md` for the real thing on real passages.

> This is a marketing paragraph in the professional register. It's clear enough to follow, but it reads like the institution wrote it, not a person, and the point arrives late.
>
> **Buried lead.** The actual claim, that the migration cut deployment time in half, sits in sentence four. Everything before it is throat-clearing about the category. Start on the claim and let the context follow.
>
> **Concept nouns, no people.** "The realization of efficiency improvements was achieved through automation." Nobody's doing anything here; it's all abstract nouns hung on "was achieved." Put a subject back in: who automated what, and what happened.
>
> **Qualifier doing no work.** "This can potentially help teams…": "potentially" hedges a claim you've already made. If it helps, say it helps.
>
> Want me to take a pass at it, or revise from these notes?

## Notes on tone in the critique itself

The critique is written to the person who wrote the piece, so keep it direct, warm, plain, sentence case. Don't perform enthusiasm and don't pad with caveats. When something works, say it plainly and move on; not every good line needs praising. When something's broken, name it without softening it into vagueness; the kindness is in the precision, not in the hedging. If the user has stated style preferences, follow them in the critique prose.
