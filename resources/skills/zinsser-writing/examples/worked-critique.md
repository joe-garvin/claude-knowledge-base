# Worked critique

Two short passages, each followed by the itemized diagnosis the skill would produce in default mode. Both passages are constructed for this example (not drawn from any book or real draft), built to carry genuine, diagnosable problems. The point is to model how a critique reads: what gets flagged, in what order, and where the line sits between flagging and fixing.

Read these to calibrate. Notice that neither critique rewrites the passage. Each names the problem and points; the cut stays with the writer.

## Passage one: professional / marketing register

A draft paragraph for a B2B cloud piece:

> In today's rapidly evolving digital landscape, organizations are increasingly seeking to leverage cloud infrastructure in order to facilitate the optimization of their operational workflows. The implementation of a robust migration strategy is something that can potentially enable teams to realize significant efficiency gains. Our solution was designed with the goal of empowering businesses to seamlessly navigate this complex transition. It's not just about moving data — it's about transforming how work gets done.

### The diagnosis

This is the marketing register, and it's a near-perfect storm of the things Zinsser's business-writing chapter warns about: the institution is writing, not a person, and there's almost no actual information in four sentences.

**No information yet, abstraction all the way down.** Read the paragraph and ask what it actually tells you: what the product is, what it does, for whom, with what result. None of that is here. It's a stack of abstractions (landscape, optimization, efficiency gains, transition) with nothing concrete underneath. This is the first problem because it's the one that matters; the line-level fixes below are secondary to "say something." Zinsser: the reader identifies with people and specifics, not with abstractions.

**Throat-clearing opener.** "In today's rapidly evolving digital landscape" is the textbook dead opener; it tells the reader nothing and delays the start. Cut it and start on the actual subject.

**Clutter: laborious phrases pushing out short words.** "In order to facilitate the optimization of" is six words doing the work of "to streamline" or just "to speed up." "The implementation of a robust migration strategy is something that can" is a long throat-clear around "a good migration can." Zinsser's rough figure applies: this paragraph could lose half its words without losing a single idea, because most of the words aren't carrying ideas.

**Concept nouns, no people, no verbs.** "The implementation of… is something that can enable teams to realize gains." Nobody does anything in that sentence; it runs on "is" and a chain of abstract nouns. Turn it around so a subject performs an action: a team migrates, deployment speeds up, costs drop. Get people and live verbs back in.

**Fad words throwing dust in the reader's eyes.** "Leverage," "robust," "seamlessly," "navigate," "empowering," "transforming": these are exactly what Zinsser calls the jargon that throws dust in the reader's eyes. They sound like work being described without any work being shown. Reach for the plain verb that says what actually happens.

**The closing construction.** "It's not just about moving data — it's about transforming how work gets done" gestures at significance instead of stating a result. What does the work actually look like afterward? Say that.

The honest summary: there's no draft here to tighten yet, only a frame. The direction isn't "cut these words" so much as "decide what one true, specific thing this paragraph is claiming, and write that." Once there's a real claim, the clutter pass has something to clean.

## Passage two: personal / literary register

A paragraph from a memoir draft, about a father:

> My father was a man who was always working with his hands, and it was something that I really admired about him quite a lot. He would spend many long hours out in the garage, and he was the kind of person who could fix virtually anything that happened to be broken. Looking back on it now, I think that those moments were actually some of the most meaningful and important times that we spent together as father and son, in a sense.

### The diagnosis

This is the personal register, and the feeling underneath it is real; the problem is that the clutter is holding the reader at arm's length from it. Unlike passage one, there's genuine material here; it just needs the dead weight pulled off so the father comes through. In this register the voice can run at full strength, so the long sentences aren't the issue. The empty qualifiers are.

**Qualifiers diluting the feeling.** "I really admired about him quite a lot." "Actually some of the most meaningful and important." "In a sense." These are the little hedges Zinsser says whittle away the reader's trust, and they're the empty kind, not the load-bearing kind that marks real doubt. They don't track genuine uncertainty; they soften a true thing until it goes vague. Removing them doesn't change a single claim, which is the test that they were clutter. Be direct about the admiration; the directness is what makes it land.

**"Was a man who was…": the long way to a simple statement.** "My father was a man who was always working with his hands" is a five-word wind-up ("was a man who was") around "my father was always working with his hands," or better, "my father worked with his hands." Same with "he was the kind of person who could fix" → "he could fix." These aren't load-bearing long sentences; they're padding that happens to be long. Zinsser's people-and-verbs point: let the father do the action directly.

**A flat verb where the detail is waiting.** "He could fix virtually anything that happened to be broken" is the sentence with the most life in it, and it's the most abstract. "Anything broken" is a category, not an image. The memoir chapter's instruction is to reach for the vivid concrete fact: the specific thing on the workbench, the one repair you remember. You don't have to write it now, but that's where the paragraph wants to go: from "anything" to the one real thing.

**The hedged ending.** "Some of the most meaningful and important times… in a sense" closes on a qualifier, which drains exactly the moment that should land hardest. Zinsser on endings: the last words are the ones that stay in the reader's ear. "In a sense" is the worst possible thing to leave ringing there. When you've earned the statement, make it flat and let it stop.

The shape of the fix: this is mostly a subtraction job. The feeling and the material are already here; pulling the qualifiers and the "was a man who was" padding will bring the father closer to the reader without your having to add anything. That's the simplicity principle doing what it's actually for: not flattening the voice, but clearing what's in front of it.

## What to notice across both

The two critiques run differently on purpose. The marketing passage had no real content under the clutter, so the lead item was "there's nothing here yet" and the direction pointed back to deciding the claim. The memoir passage had real content the clutter was obscuring, so the work was subtraction, and the voice stayed untouched; only the empty qualifiers and the padding came off. Same principles, different prescription, because the register and the underlying material differ. That's the register read from `register-notes.md` doing its job before a single line gets flagged.
