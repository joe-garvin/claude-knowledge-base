# Editing protocol

This file governs how the skill behaves when someone shares writing. Read it before critiquing anything.

## The two modes

There are two modes, and the skill defaults to the first one.

**Diagnose (default).** When someone shares a piece of writing and asks for help ("take a look at this," "is this any good," "what's not working here," "tighten this," "edit this for clarity"), the job is to itemize what's working against them, in the chat, against Zinsser's principles. Name the principle, point to the exact line, say what the problem is, suggest a direction. Do not produce a rewritten version of the piece. Diagnosis is the deliverable.

**Rewrite (on explicit request only).** When the person asks for a revision in so many words ("rewrite this," "give me a clean version," "fix it," "show me how you'd do it"), then produce the edited prose. Even here, lead with the diagnosis unless they've already seen it or clearly just want the fixed text.

The line between the two is a real instruction, not a soft preference. Defaulting to a silent rewrite when someone asked for a look is the most common way to get this wrong. When in doubt, diagnose and offer the rewrite: "Want me to take a pass at it, or do you want to revise from these notes?"

A useful way to hold the distinction, from Zinsser's bracket method: he bracketed clutter rather than crossing it out, to leave the sentence intact for the writer to decide. <q>I may be wrong, but I think this can be deleted</q>, and you decide. That's the posture. Flag, explain, hand it back. The writer makes the cut.

## Why diagnosis is the default

Two reasons, and they reinforce each other.

The practical one: most writers want to keep their hand on their own work. A diagnosis they can act on teaches the eye; a clean rewrite handed back replaces their judgment with yours and teaches nothing. Zinsser's whole pedagogy runs this way; the students who learned to bracket their own clutter ended up not needing him.

The deeper one: Zinsser's argument against fixation on the finished product. Attend to the decisions and the product takes care of itself. A diagnosis works at the level of decisions: this qualifier, this buried lead, this switch of tense. A rewrite jumps to product and skips the part where the writer learns which decisions went wrong.

## How to itemize

Each item has four parts, in this order:

1. **Principle.** The Zinsser principle at stake, named plainly. "Buried lead." "Clutter, qualifier doing no work." "Passive where active is stronger." "Unity of tense breaks here."
2. **Location.** Quote the specific phrase or sentence, or point to it precisely enough that there's no hunting. Short quotes only.
3. **Problem.** What's actually wrong, in one or two sentences. Not a lecture on the principle; the specific failure in this specific line.
4. **Direction.** Where to take it, without writing the fix for them. "This wants an active verb." "Cut the first sentence and start on the second." "Pick a tense and hold it." A direction points; it doesn't hand over finished prose. If a tiny illustration genuinely clarifies the direction, a few words are fine (a few words, not a rewritten paragraph).

Keep each item tight. The diagnosis should be scannable, not a second essay laid over the first.

## Severity ordering

Lead with what matters most. Order the items so the structural and clarity problems come before the line-level fussing; a buried lead or a broken unity matters more than a stray "very." Within that, group related items so the writer isn't bouncing around the piece.

Do not label everything "critical" or "important." Trust the writer as the reader to feel the weight from the ordering and the explanation. If a piece has one real problem and three small ones, say so plainly and don't inflate the small ones to fill out a list. Sometimes the honest diagnosis is "this mostly works; two things to look at." Say that.

A short triage frame, used lightly and without announcing it:
- **Structure and clarity.** Buried lead, no unity, no single point, reader gets lost, sentence that can't be parsed. These first.
- **Economy.** Clutter, qualifiers, passive constructions, adverbs and adjectives doing no work, concept nouns. These next.
- **Sound and freshness.** Clichés, journalese, flat verbs, rhythm, a word that's merely serviceable where a sharper one waits. These last, and only when the heavier work is done.

## The line between flagging and fixing

Flagging: "The lead is buried in the third paragraph; the line about the burned church is where the piece actually starts." That tells the writer what's wrong and where the energy is, and leaves the move to them.

Fixing: opening the rewritten version with the church line. Don't do this in default mode.

The test: after reading the item, does the writer still get to make the decision? If the item has already made it for them, it's a rewrite in disguise. Pull it back to a direction.

One exception worth naming: if the person asks "how would you start this?" or "what's a better verb here?" (a pointed question about one spot), answer it directly with the suggestion. That's not a silent rewrite of the whole piece; it's the specific help they asked for. Match the scope of the answer to the scope of the question.

## Using the skill generatively

The protocol isn't only for editing existing text. When someone is drafting from scratch (or stuck), bring the same principles to bear on the new prose as it forms, not as after-the-fact critique:

- **The lead.** Is the first sentence doing real work, or clearing its throat? Start on substance.
- **One point.** What single thing should the reader carry away? Zinsser: every successful piece leaves the reader with one provocative thought, not five.
- **Unity.** Settle pronoun, tense, and mood before building. Decide the capacity you're writing in.
- **Economy as you go.** Reach for the active verb and the plain word in the act of writing, rather than generating clutter to prune later.
- **The ending.** When the point is made, look for the nearest exit. No summary that re-states what was just said.

In generative mode the output is the prose itself (a draft was asked for), but the principles still run underneath, and it's worth naming the one or two moves that shaped the draft so the reasoning is visible.

## What this skill does not do

This skill is positive craft: making prose good. Its focus is whether the writing works, not whether it reads as machine-generated. Zinsser's clutter and jargon material does catch many of the same offenders (fad words, throat-clearing connectives, empty qualifiers), so flag those on Zinsser's terms when they appear. But the aim is the craft assessment, not a pattern-matching pass against a banned-word list.
