# Unity and structure

How a piece holds together: the unities, the lead, the ending, bit-by-bit construction, and rewriting. Read this when the diagnosis is structural: the piece is losing the reader, has no center, starts in the wrong place, or won't stop.

## Unity is the anchor

Unity is what keeps the reader from straggling off and satisfies the subconscious need for order. Before writing, settle the unities and then hold them. Zinsser names three choices:

**Unity of pronoun.** First person as participant, third as observer, occasionally second. Pick one and stay.

**Unity of tense.** Most writing runs in past, some agreeably in present. You can move among tenses to handle time (that's what tenses are for), but choose the one you principally address the reader in, and don't switch back and forth carelessly.

**Unity of mood.** Casual, or formal, or ironic, or amused: any tone is acceptable, but don't mix two or three in a way that leaves the reader unsure who's talking. Zinsser's example is the travel piece that lurches between a real person on a real trip and a guidebook reciting passport requirements. The material ends up controlling the writer instead of the writer controlling the material.

When a draft feels off in a way that isn't clutter, broken unity is often the cause: a tense that slips, a mood that shifts from personal to institutional mid-paragraph. Name which unity broke and where.

## Think small; decide the one point

Two pre-writing decisions Zinsser calls especially important:

**Reduce before you start.** Nobody writes a piece "about" something whole. Tolstoy didn't write about war and peace in general; he made reductive choices. Decide what corner of the subject to bite off, cover it well, and stop. Often you'll find you've said most of what you wanted to say about the whole by doing one part properly. This is also morale: an unwieldy scope drains the writer, and the reader feels the zest ebb.

**One point.** Every successful piece should leave the reader with a single provocative thought they didn't have before, not two, not five. Decide what that one point is; it shapes route, destination, tone, and attitude.

The four pre-flight questions Zinsser poses before starting (useful as a generative checklist):
- In what capacity am I addressing the reader? (Reporter? Provider of information? Ordinary person?)
- What pronoun and tense?
- What style? (Impersonal reportorial? Personal but formal? Personal and casual?)
- What attitude toward the material? (Involved? Detached? Ironic? Amused?)
And then the two that matter most: how much do I want to cover, and what one point do I want to make?

## Trust your material

A counterweight to all the planning: once writing, if the material leads somewhere you didn't intend but the vibrations are good, follow it. The act of writing generates thoughts you couldn't have planned. Don't become the prisoner of a preconceived outline. <q>Writing is no respecter of blueprints.</q> If the second half then clashes with the first, you at least know which half is truest, and you go back and rebuild the rest to match.

This sits naturally with how associative, accumulative drafting often works: finding the thesis by writing toward it. The structural discipline (unities, one point) is the frame; trusting the material is the permission to let the frame bend when the writing knows better.

## The lead

<q>The most important sentence in any article is the first one.</q> If it doesn't pull the reader to the second, the piece is dead, and the same for the second to the third. The lead is that chain of sentences, each tugging the reader forward until hooked.

How long? No formula; some leads hook in a sentence, some amble for pages. The only test is whether it works. What the lead must do:

- **Capture immediately.** Cajole with freshness, novelty, paradox, humor, surprise, an unusual idea, an arresting fact, a question. Anything that nudges curiosity and tugs the sleeve.
- **Then do real work.** Provide the hard detail that tells the reader why the piece exists and why they should care, without dwelling on the reason. Keep them curious.
- **Build.** Every paragraph amplifies the last. Give special care to the final sentence of each paragraph: the springboard to the next.

Narrative is the oldest hook; everybody wants to be told a story. Zinsser admires leads that start *whap*, right in the action, with no stage-setting (the Dead Sea Scrolls discovery opening on a Bedouin boy throwing a stone into a cave). The principle: start on substance, no throat-clearing.

## The ending

Knowing when to stop matters more than most writers think, and the last sentence deserves nearly as much thought as the first. The failure mode Zinsser names by sound: the piece that reaches a series of perfect conclusions that never conclude, like a sermon that won't end.

The trap with a name: **the summary ending**. The signal is a sentence forming that begins "In sum, it can be noted that…" or "What insights, then, have we gleaned…": the compressed restatement of what was already said in detail. The reader hears the cranking, feels the tension sag, and quits.

The rule: <q>when you're ready to stop, stop.</q> When the facts are presented and the point is made, find the nearest exit. The best ending takes the reader slightly by surprise and yet seems exactly right, like a curtain line: the scene over a beat before expected and the rightness landing after. Zinsser's own favorite moves: bring the piece full circle to echo a note from the opening, or end on a good quotation. But the governing instruction is just: stop when you're done, and make the last sentence one that lingers.

## Bit-by-bit construction and rewriting

Zinsser's strongest methodological claim: **rewriting is the essence of writing**. Professional writers rewrite and then rewrite what they've rewritten. Most rewriting isn't drafting fresh versions; it's reshaping, tightening, and refining the raw first try, and above all making sure the reader has a narrative flow to follow with no trouble from start to finish.

<q>Writing is an evolving process, not a product.</q> Nobody gets it right the first time. The watch should run smoothly and have no extra parts.

What rewriting actually attends to (the questions to keep asking, which also drive a good diagnosis):
- Is there something the reader needed early in the sentence that's stranded at the end?
- When the reader starts sentence B, do they know you've shifted (subject, tense, tone, emphasis) from sentence A? (Zinsser's example: "The tragic hero of the play is Othello. Small and malevolent, Iago feeds his jealous suspicions." The name in the reader's ear is Othello, so the second sentence misfires.)
- Does each sentence lead cleanly out of the one before?
- What's the last word of the sentence? It's the one that stays in the reader's ear; put the word you want to land there.

The mechanical practice that catches all of this: **read it aloud, start to finish, holding in mind where you left the reader in the previous sentence.** The ear hears the lost reader, the repeated thought, the missing link.

Zinsser's bracketed before/after paragraph (a clear first-draft paragraph annotated sentence by sentence with an editor's thoughts, then revised) is the direct model for the itemized critique format. The `examples/` file adapts that method across registers. The move is the same: name what each sentence does wrong and where it should go, in order, and let the rebuild follow from the notes.
