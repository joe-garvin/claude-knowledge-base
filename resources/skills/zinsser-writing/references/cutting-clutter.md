# Cutting clutter

Zinsser's most tactical material: the specific patterns of clutter and how to remove them. Read this when the diagnosis is about economy (words doing no work). Pair it with the economy items in the editing protocol.

## The bracket method

Zinsser's device from his Yale teaching: put brackets around every component that isn't doing useful work, rather than crossing it out. The brackets say *this can probably go and the meaning won't change; read the sentence without it and see*. The decision stays with the writer.

This is the model for the editing protocol's whole posture. When diagnosing clutter, you're bracketing: pointing at what looks deletable and explaining why, leaving the cut to the writer. His students reported the brackets followed them through life. The goal is to install the eye, not to do the pruning for them.

His summary figure, worth holding as a rough target: most first drafts can be cut by about half without losing information or the writer's voice.

## The clutter catalog

The specific patterns Zinsser names. These are what to look for.

**The laborious phrase that pushed out the short word.** "At this point in time" for *now*. "Currently" / "at the present time" for *now*. The long Latinate word where a short Anglo-Saxon one means the same: assistance/help, numerous/many, facilitate/ease, individual/man or woman, remainder/rest, initial/first, implement/do, sufficient/enough, attempt/try, referred to as/called. Short is generally better than long.

**Prepositions draped onto verbs that don't need them.** Head *up* a committee, free *up* time, face *up* to problems. The verb already does the work; the preposition is dead weight. "Order up," "step down" (resign? retire? get fired? be precise).

**The "experiencing" family, pomposity that blunts the truth.** The dentist who asks if you're experiencing pain instead of "does it hurt." Inflated phrasing makes the writer sound important and dulls the edge of what's actually being said.

**Word clusters that explain how we're about to explain.** "I might add," "it should be pointed out," "it is interesting to note." Zinsser's reply: if you might add, add it; if it should be pointed out, point it out; if it's interesting, make it interesting. These announce content instead of delivering it.

**Phrases that inflate what needs no inflating.** "With the possible exception of" (except), "due to the fact that" (because), "he totally lacked the ability to" (he couldn't), "until such time as" (until), "for the purpose of" (for).

**Fad words and jargon.** Paradigm, parameter, prioritize, potentialize, leverage, robust, seamless, synergy. Every profession grows its own arsenal to throw dust in the reader's eyes. In business and tech writing this is a live hazard; the surrounding material is dense with it. (See `register-notes.md`; the professional register is where jargon pressure is highest and where Zinsser's business-writing chapter applies most directly.)

## Adverbs that duplicate the verb

Most adverbs are unnecessary. The radio "blared loudly": blare already means loud. Teeth "clenched tightly": there's no other way to clench. "Effortlessly easy," "totally flabbergasted" (you can't be partly flabbergasted). When a verb carries the meaning, the adverb just clutters and weakens. Choose the precise verb instead and let it stand alone.

Zinsser singles out the slippery evaluative adverbs: "decidedly," "eminently," "arguably," "virtually." They gesture at a judgment without making one. Is the pitcher the best on the team, provably? Then drop "arguably." Is it open to argument? Then say so. The adverb that does neither is fog.

## Adjectives that state what's already known

Same disease. "Tall skyscraper," "yellow daffodils," "brownish dirt": the noun already carries it. Adjectives sown in by habit to make prose lusher (stately elms, frisky kittens, sleepy lagoons) are self-indulgence for the writer and burden for the reader. Not every oak has to be gnarled.

The rule: make the adjective do work the noun isn't already doing. "Garish" daffodils is a judgment the noun doesn't carry, so keep it. "Red dirt" where the dirt is genuinely red and it matters, keep it. The decorative adjective, cut it.

## Little qualifiers

The small hedges: "a bit," "a little," "sort of," "kind of," "rather," "quite," "very," "too," "pretty much," "in a sense." They dilute force and persuasiveness. Don't be a bit confused and sort of tired; be confused, be tired. <q>Good writing is lean and confident.</q>

Zinsser frames the cost as trust: every little qualifier whittles away a fraction of the reader's confidence in the writer. Don't be kind of bold; be bold.

**One important distinction.** Not every qualifier is clutter. There's a difference between the empty hedge and the qualifier that tracks genuine uncertainty:

- The **empty qualifier** adds nothing: the timid hedge, the intensifier insisting on legitimacy ("genuine," "real," "actual" used to assert rather than describe). Cut these freely.
- **Qualification that marks real doubt** is load-bearing. "I don't think it's so much X as Y" can be doing honest work: holding two true things in tension, marking a claim the writer isn't fully sure of. That's not Zinsserian hedging; it carries meaning.

The test before flagging a qualifier: is it timidity (cut) or is it tracking real uncertainty (keep)? If removing it changes nothing about what's claimed, it was clutter. If removing it makes the sentence claim more confidence than the writer actually has, it was doing work.

## Concept nouns and creeping nounism

Nouns that name a concept where a verb would tell what someone did. "The common reaction is incredulous laughter" has no people and no working verb, only "is." Turn it around: "Most people just laugh with disbelief." Get people doing things; get real verbs back in. The clue is a sentence built on "is"/"isn't" plus abstract nouns (reaction, cynicism, response, hostility) with nobody visible performing anything.

*Creeping nounism* is the related disease: stringing nouns together where one noun or verb would do. "Money problem areas," "precipitation activity," and Zinsser's prize specimen, "communication facilitation skills development intervention," not a person or a working verb in sight. This is endemic in corporate and institutional writing, so it's a live target in the professional register specifically.

## Overstatement

Don't inflate. The party that left the living room "looking as if an atomic bomb had gone off." The novice's hyperbole exhausts the reader and spends credibility. Once the reader catches one bogus exaggeration, everything else is suspect. Let humor sneak up; don't oversell it.

## Punctuation, briefly

Zinsser's punctuation notes that bear on cutting:

- **The period.** Most writers don't reach it soon enough. A sentence hopelessly mired is usually trying to hold two dissimilar thoughts, so break it. (The exception: a genuinely load-bearing long sentence under full control is fine. Zinsser allows this too: "if you want to write long sentences, be a genius… or at least make sure the sentence is under control from beginning to end.")
- **The dash.** Zinsser's friend, not a bumpkin. Two uses: a single dash to amplify or justify the first half of a sentence in the second, and paired dashes to set off a parenthetical thought. (If the user has a house rule about dashes, hold them to their own standard when diagnosing their output, not Zinsser's more permissive line.)
- **The semicolon.** A Victorian mustiness hangs over it; use sparingly; it slows momentum to a 19th-century pace. Where a writer leans on it as a structural crutch, flag it and suggest the dash or a clean break.
- **The exclamation point.** Don't use it to tell the reader something was funny or exciting; it robs them of finding it so themselves. Construct the sentence so the emphasis lands without it.
