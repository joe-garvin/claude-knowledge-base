# The human element

Voice, taste, enjoyment, confidence: the attitudes Zinsser puts in Part IV, the part he says he didn't know enough to write until the sixth edition. Read this when the work is technically clean but lifeless, or when someone is drafting and the question is less "is this correct" than "does this sound like a person who means it."

## One voice, not a voice per subject

Zinsser wrote a book on baseball and a book on jazz and refused to write one in "sports English" and the other in "jazz English." He wrote both in his own voice, because he wanted readers to know they were hearing from the same person. <q>Don't alter your voice to fit your subject.</q> Develop one voice readers recognize on the page.

This principle needs careful handling for any writer who works across registers (personal/literary and professional/business), because the skill must serve both. The reconciliation lives in full in `register-notes.md`. The short version: "one voice" is true. The constant is the person, the plain vocabulary, the trust in the reader, the aversion to performance. What flexes between registers is not voice but *register*: how much gets explained, how formal the frame, how much the personal "I" surfaces. Zinsser himself models this; his business-writing chapter and his memoir chapter are recognizably the same writer at different distances from the material.

## The effortless style is strenuously achieved

The relaxed, talking-to-you style (E. B. White's) reads as if the words just tumbled out. The opposite is true. The effortless style is the product of strenuous effort and constant refining; the grammar is formal, the words plain and precise, the cadences worked. White's sentence about the ailing pig looks like a man in a rocking chair, but nothing in it is accidental.

The warning attached: don't confuse the effortless style with the **breezy** style. Breeziness is the writer trying to be a pal (cheap slang, winks, "you see," exclamation points, philosophizing), and it's actually *harder* to read than good English, because it litters the path with obstacles. Readers stop reading when they feel talked down to. Write with respect for the language at its best and for the reader at their best. (Zinsser's test, again: read it aloud and see if you like the sound of your own voice.)

For the business register this is the specific trap to watch; the pressure to sound approachable can tip into breeziness. The fix isn't formality; it's the disciplined plain style, warm because it's clear, not because it's matey.

## Taste: knowing what to omit

Voice that readers enjoy is largely a matter of taste, and taste is mostly **knowing what not to do**. Two equally skilled jazz pianists: the one with taste puts every note to work telling the story; the one without drowns it in ripples. Same for the painter, the designer (design is the servant of the written word, not its decoration).

The clearest place taste shows is clichés. <q>Clichés are the enemy of taste.</q> They're the familiar friends waiting to do your thinking for you, and even careful writers let a few through on the first draft. That's exactly why you listen for them on the rewrite, reading aloud, where they sound incriminating. Taste reaches for the word with surprise, strength, and precision; non-taste reaches for the corny synonym ("top brass," "umpteenth," "zillions"), which is also imprecise. Call people what they are: officials, directors, presidents, not "the powers that be."

## Writing for yourself

The audience principle from Part I belongs here too as an attitude: you are writing primarily to please yourself. If it amuses you, put it in (only you can); it can always come out later. Go about it with enjoyment and you'll reach the readers worth reaching. This isn't license for self-indulgence (see ego vs. egotism in `register-notes.md` and the memoir material); the craft still has to hold. It's permission to stop writing for an imagined teacher or editor, which is the surest way to write for nobody.

## Enjoyment, fear, confidence

Zinsser's credo, taken from the old *Herald Tribune*: a sense of enjoyment is a priceless attribute, and the reader can feel it. S. J. Perelman's line, which Zinsser says stated the whole case: <q>The reader has to feel that the writer is feeling good.</q> And then Perelman's addition: "even if he isn't." The writer jump-starts at the moment of performance, like an actor or musician. You turn on the switch; nobody does it for you.

Against enjoyment runs **fear**, planted early, usually at school, never quite gone. The blank screen freezes the writer into no words or bad words. Zinsser's consolation is structural: you get another shot tomorrow, and the next day. <q>With each rewrite I try to force my personality onto the material.</q> The personality and the confidence aren't prerequisites for the first draft; they're what the rewriting installs.

How this bears on the skill: when a writer is stuck or a draft is flat, the diagnosis often isn't a clutter problem; it's that the writer isn't yet present in the prose. The move is to find the sentence where a real person shows up (Zinsser's editorial instinct: throw away the ornamental opening and start where the human appears) and build from there. Confidence in the prose reads as authority; hedging reads as its absence. This connects to the qualifier material in `cutting-clutter.md` ("be bold," not "be kind of bold"), but here the frame is attitude, not economy.

## The tyranny of the final product

The deepest of the Part IV attitudes, and the one that underwrites the whole editing protocol. Writers fixate on the finished piece (the byline, the layout, the sale), and that fixation deflects them from the earlier decisions that actually determine shape, voice, and content. Zinsser's premise: <q>If the process is sound, the product will take care of itself.</q>

The practical consequence for this skill: a good diagnosis works at the level of *decisions* (this buried lead, this broken unity, this dead verb), not at the level of *product* (here's a clean rewrite). Attending to the decisions is how the writer learns; handing over the product skips the learning. This is why the skill defaults to diagnosis; it's the tyranny-of-the-final-product principle applied to the act of editing itself.
